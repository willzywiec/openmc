Yes, with one wrinkle worth being precise about.

Speed and kinetic energy carry the same information for a neutron — they're
related by E = ½mv² (nonrelativistic, which is fine up to ~10 MeV; even at
14 MeV the relativistic correction is under 1%). Specifying one specifies the
other. So (E, μ) and (v, μ) are equivalent parameterizations of the velocity
vector's magnitude-and-direction.

The wrinkle: the full velocity vector **v** has three components, not two. In
spherical symmetry you've collapsed the angular part from (θ, φ) to just μ
because rotational symmetry around r̂ kills the azimuthal dependence — but
only for the *response*, not for individual neutrons. Any single neutron has
a real azimuthal angle φ; it's just that the Green's function averaged over φ
is φ-independent, so you don't need to track it as an input coordinate.

So the source phase space for your emulator is genuinely 3D: **(r, μ, E)**,
or equivalently **(r, μ, v)**. Direction (μ) and speed (E or v) together
specify the velocity vector up to the symmetry-killed azimuth.

One thing worth flagging since you're thinking about this as a Green's
function: the *response* phase space is higher-dimensional than the source
phase space, even with spherical symmetry. A neutron born at (r, μ, E) and
observed later at (r', μ', E', t) — that's 4 coordinates plus time. The
Green's function G(r, μ, E → r', μ', E', t) is a map from a 3D source
manifold to a 4D response manifold. So when you discretize the output grid,
that's where the dimensional cost shows up.

For Godiva specifically, energy spans roughly 1 keV to 15 MeV in fast-system
problems, and you'd want log-spaced binning because cross-sections vary by
orders of magnitude across that range. Direction μ ∈ [−1, +1] is naturally
uniform. Radius r ∈ [0, R_Godiva ≈ 8.7 cm] depends on whether you care about
edge effects — sources very near the surface behave qualitatively differently
than core sources because they leak before fissioning.

The progression has six rough phases, and I want to be honest about where the

**Phase 1** is what we've been talking about — build the stochastic Green's

conditional mean but the tail behavior, the variance structure, and the
rare-event geometry of the realization distribution. Success criterion: the
model reproduces the empirical chain-length distribution out to the tail, not
just the bulk. This phase is a transport methodology paper on its own.

**Phase 2** is probing the latent geometry. Once the model has learned a
coordinate system on realization-space, you ask what's there. Are there
identifiable regions of latent space corresponding to "explosive"
trajectories — long chains, deep branching, late-time fission events? Is the
manifold low-dimensional or fractal? Do near-critical configurations show
clustering or self-similarity? You're treating the trained generative model
as a measurement instrument for branching-tree statistics, the same way
physicists treat a detector. This is where you start finding empirical
objects that have analytical analogs in Le Jan–Sznitman.

**Phase 3** is the methodological move that makes the bridge to NS
legitimate. Raw neutron transport branching is sign-definite — a fission
event always produces ν > 0 neutrons, no cancellation. The NS tree expansion
has signed contributions because the pressure projection and the
incompressibility constraint create cancellation between trees. So you have
to engineer signed observables in the transport case that mirror this
structure. One natural candidate: differences between adjoint-weighted and
forward-weighted flux contributions, where the adjoint weighting introduces
an effective sign by virtue of the importance function. Another: directional
asymmetries (forward minus backward leakage) that can take either sign. The
point is to manufacture, in a tractable system, an object whose summability
question has the same character as the NS one.

**Phase 4** is the resummation experiment. Compute the tree expansion to high
order — meaning, decompose your generative model's outputs by branching
depth and look at how contributions accumulate. Does the partial-sum sequence
behave like an asymptotic series with a well-defined Borel transform? Does it
diverge at fixed order but resum to something finite? This is the empirical
test of the central conjecture from the original sketch. If transport tree
expansions show Borel summability in the near-critical regime, that's strong
circumstantial evidence the method should work for NS. If they don't — if
you find the expansion genuinely diverges in a non-resummable way — that's
also informative, and probably means something about the NS strategy needs
to change.

**Phase 5** is the translation to Le Jan–Sznitman. By this point you have
intuition and methodological tools developed in a tractable setting. The
work here is conceptual transfer: figuring out which features of the
transport tree statistics are universal (driven by branching-process
structure alone) versus which are specific to neutron physics (driven by
cross-sections, fission multiplicity distributions, etc.). The universal
features are the ones that should carry over. The vortex-ring numerical
experiment we talked about originally is the natural Phase 5 deliverable —
the same kind of generative emulation, but on a 3D NS configuration, with
the methodology shaken out on Godiva.

**Phase 6** is the actual theorem, which is the part where you stop doing
experiments and start writing proofs. The empirical work doesn't substitute
for analysis; it tells you what's true so you know what to prove. This is
the longest phase by far, the one where most of the actual mathematical
effort lives, and the one where I have the least to offer beyond pointing at
the existing PDE literature.

That changes the framing significantly, and it makes the project more
grounded — and more fundable — than the NS angle alone.

POI is fundamentally a tail-statistics question. Standard k_eff and α tell
you the *mean* behavior of the neutron population. POI tells you the
probability that a single source neutron, or a small Poisson-distributed
source, initiates a divergent chain rather than dying out. For a
near-critical or slightly supercritical system, the *expected* population
grows but most individual chains still die from early stochastic loss — you
need many seed neutrons before you reliably get initiation, and the exact
statistics of "reliably" are what POI quantifies. This is exactly the object
that requires the full distribution over realizations, not the mean. Your
gut connecting Green's function to POI is right.

The standard POI machinery — Hansen's work, Bell's treatment in *Nuclear
Reactor Theory*, the more recent stuff coming out of LANL on weapons physics
applications — uses the backward (adjoint) Boltzmann equation with a
multiplicative source term to derive a nonlinear integro-differential
equation for the *probability generating function* of the descendant
population. The probability of initiation is then 1 minus the value of this
PGF at zero. This is the Bell equation for the persistence probability, and
it has a well-known structure: the nonlinearity comes from the fission
multiplicity distribution (specifically the factorial moments
⟨ν(ν−1)…⟩), and the equation is solved either deterministically on a
phase-space grid or by Monte Carlo with a population-tracking estimator.

The connection to what you've been describing is direct. The Bell PGF
equation is exactly the deterministic shadow of the branching process — the
quantity it computes is a functional of the realization distribution,
specifically the probability that the realization tree goes extinct. If you
have a generative model that captures the full conditional distribution of
realizations given source (r, μ, E), then POI is just the integral of the
indicator-of-extinction over your learned distribution. You're computing the
same object the Bell equation computes, but as a statistic of an emulated
realization distribution rather than as the solution of a nonlinear PDE.

This reframes your phases. Phase 1 isn't really "build a stochastic Green's
function for Godiva" anymore — it's "build a POI emulator that gives you the
full POI(r, μ, E) function plus its uncertainty structure, validated against
direct MC and against Bell-equation solutions where available." That's an
immediately useful object for criticality safety, weapons physics, and NEST
diagnostic work. The Green's function framing is a *consequence* of doing
this right — once you have the conditional distribution, you can extract
POI, mean response, variance, higher moments, and tail statistics, all from
the same trained model.

A few specific things this clarifies.

The "near-critical" emphasis I was pushing earlier becomes even sharper. POI
is most informative — and most computationally annoying — exactly in the
regime where the system is barely supercritical. Below criticality POI is
identically zero in the absence of an external source; deeply supercritical
it saturates near 1 for any seed; the interesting variation lives in the
narrow window around α ≈ 0 where the chain probability is sensitively
dependent on source location, energy, and direction. Godiva sits there. So
does any device that matters operationally.

Direction (μ) matters for POI in a way it doesn't for k_eff. A neutron born
inward in Godiva has a much higher POI than one born outward at the same
radius, because the inward-traveling neutron passes through more material
before potentially leaking. Standard POI tabulations often integrate over
angle to give a flux-weighted average, which destroys exactly the
information you want. A Green's-function-style emulator that retains μ as an
input gives you something the standard treatment doesn't.

The branching process pathology I mentioned earlier — variance growing
faster than mean, single lucky early fissions dominating tallies — is *the*
central computational problem in POI estimation. This is why direct MC POI
calculations need so many histories to converge, and why Bell-equation
methods are often preferred despite the deterministic-grid pain. A
well-trained generative model trained on a moderate ensemble could
potentially do better than either, by interpolating across the (r, μ, E)
space rather than running independent calculations at each source point.

The NS connection becomes more conditional but doesn't go away. The
branching tree summability question is structurally close to the Bell PGF
question — both ask whether a sum over branching histories converges. So the
technical work you do for POI is genuinely transferable. But you can succeed
completely on POI without ever closing the NS loop, which is the right kind
of hedge. POI is valuable to LLNL, NEST, and the criticality safety
community in its own right, and a generative-model-based POI tool would be

No, and the comparison is worth being precise about because S_N and a
Green's function are answering different questions.

S_N — discrete ordinates — is a deterministic method for solving the
*forward* transport equation on a discretized phase-space grid. You
discretize space, you pick a finite set of directions (the "ordinates"), you
discretize energy into groups, and you sweep the resulting linear system to
get the angular flux ψ(r, μ, E) for a *specific source*. If you change the
source, you re-solve. Each calculation gives you the response to one source
distribution. The neural operator surrogates I mentioned (DeepONet, FNO
trained on S_N data) learn the operator that maps source distributions to
flux fields — which is essentially a Green's function in disguise, because
the linear transport operator's Green's function is exactly the
source-to-flux map.

So in the linear deterministic setting, "S_N surrogate" and "Green's
function" are nearly the same object, just packaged differently. The S_N
surrogate learns G implicitly by training on (source, flux) pairs. A direct
Green's function approach would parameterize G(r, μ, E → r', μ', E')
explicitly. For mean-field quantities, both work, and the recent neural
operator papers show that surrogates can match S_N to ~100 pcm on k_eff and
high accuracy on flux fields with massive runtime savings. That's a real
result.

Where the S_N approach is genuinely better than what you're proposing: it's
mature, it has decades of verification and validation, the error is
controlled and quantifiable, and you can compute mean fluxes and k_eff to
high accuracy without doing anything stochastic. For the questions S_N is
built to answer — what's the steady-state flux, what's k_eff, what's the
response to this source — it's the right tool, and an S_N-based neural
operator surrogate is a sensible accelerator.

Where S_N is the wrong tool: anything that requires the realization
distribution rather than the mean. POI is the canonical example. S_N solves
for ψ, the mean angular flux. POI is a functional of the *probability
distribution over neutron populations*, not of the mean population. To get
POI from a deterministic method you have to solve the Bell PGF equation,
which is a different equation — it's a nonlinear integro-differential
equation for a generating function, not a linear equation for a flux. The
Bell equation has its own S_N-like discretization machinery, but it's a
separate calculation, and it's much less mature than standard S_N. Surrogate
work on Bell PGF specifically barely exists.

The same is true for any tail-statistics or rare-event question:
chain-length distributions, time-to-first-fission distributions, variance of
the descendant population, factorial moments beyond second order. These are
all properties of the realization distribution, and S_N gives you no direct
access to them. You can compute them with MC, you can compute some of them
by stacking up coupled Bell-style equations for higher moments, but you
can't get them from a single S_N calculation.

This is the structural argument for the generative-model approach over an
S_N surrogate, in the POI context: S_N is solving the wrong equation. It
solves the linear forward equation; POI requires the nonlinear backward
equation for the extinction probability, or equivalently the full
realization distribution. An S_N surrogate trained on flux data, no matter
how accurate, doesn't carry POI information — you'd have to train a
separate surrogate on Bell-equation data, and at that point you're not
using S_N anymore, you're using the Bell PGF equation.

The honest comparison would be: stochastic Green's function emulator (your
proposal) versus Bell PGF equation surrogate (the deterministic competitor
for the same target). And there the case for your approach is more
interesting:

The Bell PGF equation gives you POI as a scalar function of source phase
space, but only POI. You want higher moments, you couple in another
equation. You want the full distribution, you keep coupling. It's a
hierarchy that closes only in the limit of infinitely many equations. A
generative model trained on MC realizations gives you the full distribution
natively, and POI is just one statistic of it.

The Bell equation's nonlinearity comes from the factorial moments of the
fission distribution, and those are well-known for U-235 — so the equation
is well-posed and tractable. But the generative-model approach automatically
incorporates the actual ν distribution from your nuclear data files,
including correlations and tails that the Bell equation truncates.

The Bell equation gives you no error structure on the POI estimate beyond
grid convergence. The generative model, properly trained, gives you a
posterior over the realization distribution and therefore a credible
interval on POI that reflects both training-data uncertainty and intrinsic
stochasticity.

So the answer to your question is: for k_eff and mean flux, S_N surrogates
beat what you're proposing — they're more mature and they're the right tool
for the job. For POI and other realization-distribution functionals, the
generative-model approach is going after something neither standard S_N nor
S_N surrogates can deliver, and the natural deterministic competitor (Bell
PGF surrogate) has its own limitations that the generative approach doesn't
share.

The case for your project isn't "better than S_N." It's "S_N solves a
different problem." The right framing for a proposal or paper is: here is a
class of questions — POI, chain statistics, rare-event characterization —
for which existing surrogate work is silent because existing surrogate work
has been built on the linear forward operator, and here is a method that
addresses them directly.

Yes, and I want to lay out the alternatives honestly rather than steering
you toward what we've been discussing.

Three real options for building a POI-resolved-in-source-phase-space
capability for Godiva, ranked roughly by how mature the underlying
methodology is.

**Option 1: Direct numerical solution of the Bell PGF equation, tabulated on
(r, μ, E)**

This is the textbook approach and the one most defensible from a
methodology-acceptance standpoint. The Bell equation for the extinction
probability is a backward integro-differential equation in phase space —
you discretize (r, μ, E), pick a fission multiplicity distribution and
cross-section set, and iterate to convergence. The output is POI(r, μ, E)
directly, on a grid. Bell did this in the 1960s for simple geometries.
Modern implementations exist in the weapons community though many aren't
openly published.

The strengths: the math is settled, the equation is well-posed, the
convergence is controlled, and you can verify your implementation against
known limits (POI → 0 for k_eff → 1 from below at fixed leakage, POI → 1 −
e^(−Mε) for fully supercritical, etc.). For Godiva spherical symmetry
collapses spatial dimension to 1, so the discretized phase space is (r, μ,
E) at maybe (50, 16, 200) grid points — entirely tractable on a laptop.

The weaknesses: you commit to a specific multiplicity distribution and
cross-section treatment up front, and any uncertainty quantification has to
be done by re-solving the equation many times. No native error bars. Higher
moments require additional coupled equations. And it gives you the mean POI
on a grid, not a generative object you can sample from.

**Option 2: Direct Monte Carlo with descendant tracking, tabulated on (r, μ,
E)**

The brute-force approach. For each grid point in (r, μ, E), launch N source
neutrons in OpenMC or COG, track each chain to extinction or to some large-
multiplication cutoff, and tabulate the fraction that initiate divergent
chains. POI(r, μ, E) is then just a binomial proportion at each grid point
with native uncertainty from N.

Strengths: minimal modeling assumptions. You're using the full
continuous-energy nuclear data. You get the full distribution of chain
outcomes, not just POI — variance, tail behavior, time-to-divergence, all
available. Trivially parallel. Validates against analytic limits the same
way Option 1 does.

Weaknesses: convergence is statistically expensive in exactly the regime
you care about. Near criticality, POI is small and high-variance — you
might need 10⁶ histories per grid point to get POI to 10% relative
uncertainty. With 50×16×200 = 160,000 grid points, that's 1.6×10¹¹
histories, which is real machine time but not absurd. The bigger problem is
that you're computing 160,000 essentially independent things and getting no
interpolation between them — every new query point requires fresh
histories.

**Option 3: Generative emulator trained on Option 2 data**

What we've been discussing. Run Option 2 at a Latin-hypercube sampled subset
of (r, μ, E) points, train a conditional generative model on the realization
ensembles, query the model to get POI and any other functional you want at
any source point.

Strengths: continuous in source phase space, gives you the full realization
distribution natively, supports rare-event analysis, naturally produces
uncertainty estimates, and the trained model is fast to query for downstream
applications.

Weaknesses: methodologically novel. Validation harder. The generative
model's latent geometry has to be probed carefully to confirm it isn't
producing artifacts in the tail. And you're paying an upfront training cost
on top of the MC sampling cost — though you save on grid-point MC because
you're interpolating.

**Two more options I'd think hard about**

**Option 4: Adjoint-weighted MC.** Rather than tabulating POI on a grid,
you compute the adjoint flux ψ⁺(r, μ, E) for the extinction-probability
operator, which is itself the Green's function in the linear-response sense
for POI. This is a single MC calculation that gives you POI at every source
point simultaneously, with proper variance reduction through importance
sampling. The catch is that the Bell equation is nonlinear, so the adjoint
formulation only gives you the linearized response near a reference state —
but for a fixed configuration like Godiva, that's actually fine. The
reference state is the configuration itself, and the adjoint gives you POI
as a functional of source distribution. This is closer to standard transport
practice than any of the above and might be the right tool.

**Option 5: Polynomial chaos or Gaussian process regression on Option 2
outputs.** Instead of a generative model, fit a surrogate to the *moments*
of the realization distribution at each source point. POI is one such
moment (extinction probability). Variance, third moment, etc. each get
their own surrogate. This is less novel than the generative approach but
much more tractable and standard in the UQ community. You lose the full
distributional structure but gain a defensible methodology and faster
training.

**Honest ranking for your specific situation**

If the goal is a publishable POI capability with clear LLNL utility and
minimal risk, Option 4 (adjoint-weighted MC) is probably the right answer.
It's the natural extension of standard transport methodology, it's
defensible to reviewers in NSE or similar journals, and it gives you the
spatial/angular resolution you want without the methodological novelty
burden.

If the goal is to push the methodology forward and the POI tool is the
vehicle, Option 3 (generative emulator) is what we've been talking about
and remains genuinely novel. The risk is that you end up with a methodology
paper rather than an applications paper, which is fine if that's what you
want.

If the goal is fastest-time-to-result for a specific operational question —
say, POI for a specific configuration NEST cares about — Option 2 (direct
MC) is the right answer and you should stop building general infrastructure
and just compute the thing you need.

The fact that you keep returning to the Green's function framing suggests
Option 4 might actually be what you've been reaching for without naming it.
Adjoint flux *is* a Green's function — it's literally the response function
for the linearized POI operator. You'd get the (r, μ, E) resolution, you'd
get continuous phase-space coverage, and you'd be working with a tool the
transport community already understands. The generative-model angle could
come later as a refinement that captures higher moments the adjoint
formulation can't.

**What OpenMC has natively**

OpenMC has solid adjoint capability for k-eigenvalue problems through the
IFP (iterated fission probability) method — you've used this yourself for
your α-eigenvalue work. The IFP gives you the adjoint-weighted flux ψ⁺ for
the k-eigenvalue operator, and that adjoint is the importance function for
criticality: a neutron's contribution to the asymptotic fission population.

The critical distinction is that the IFP adjoint is the adjoint of the
*linear* k-eigenvalue equation. POI requires the adjoint of the *nonlinear*
Bell PGF equation, which is a different operator. They are related but not
identical.

For a critical or near-critical system, the linearized Bell adjoint and the
k-eigenvalue adjoint coincide to leading order. The k-eigenvalue adjoint
tells you the asymptotic descendant population from a source neutron at (r,
μ, E). POI tells you the probability that the descendant population is
nonzero. These are related by:

POI(r, μ, E) ≈ 1 − exp(−⟨N_descendants⟩) for Poisson-distributed chain
outcomes near critical, where ⟨N_descendants⟩ is proportional to the
k-eigenvalue adjoint.

This approximation is exact in the limit of many independent chain attempts
and breaks down badly for low-multiplicity systems (deep subcritical, where
most chains die immediately and the Poisson assumption fails) and for
highly correlated chains (heavy reflectors, weird geometries). For Godiva
specifically — bare HEU, fast spectrum, k_eff just below or above 1 — the
approximation is decent but not quantitative.

**What this means practically**

Step 1, which you can do today with stock OpenMC: run an IFP adjoint
calculation on Godiva and tabulate ψ⁺(r, μ, E). This gives you the
importance function, which is approximately POI up to the Poisson-conversion
step. Convergence and sampling are well-understood. You'd have a first-pass
spatial/angular/energy-resolved estimate of POI in a few weeks of work,
mostly spent on tally setup and post-processing.

Step 2, which requires more work but doesn't need a fork: post-process the
IFP adjoint with the proper nonlinear Bell conversion. This means computing
the full chain-multiplicity distribution at each source point — the e_m
distribution from Walston's Fourier method or from Böhnel — and using ψ⁺ to
set the parameter (essentially M_e or k_p) that goes into Böhnel. POI is
then 1 − e_0 from that distribution. This gives you a quantitatively correct
POI rather than the Poisson approximation. It's a Python wrapper around
OpenMC's IFP output, plus a Bell solver — call it a few months of work
including validation.

Step 3, which requires a fork: solve the actual nonlinear Bell PGF equation
directly in OpenMC, which means modifying the IFP estimator to iterate on
the extinction probability rather than the linear flux. This is the
principled solution and gives you POI without approximation, but you're
doing what you did for the α-eigenvalue capability — modifying core OpenMC
physics. Worth it if the post-processed approach in Step 2 shows systematic
errors in the regimes you care about, but probably not the right place to
start.

**The Bell-equation-on-a-grid alternative**

You could also skip OpenMC entirely for the POI computation and solve the
discretized Bell equation directly on (r, μ, E) using your existing
transport infrastructure to compute the cross-section and kernel inputs.
This is less elegant but more transparent — you'd have a simple Python
implementation of the Bell PGF iteration, validated against limits, with
OpenMC providing the underlying nuclear data and transport kernels. For a
1D spherical Godiva problem this is a few hundred lines of code.

The downside is that you're maintaining a parallel piece of infrastructure
rather than leveraging OpenMC's machinery. The upside is that the math is
fully transparent and you can do anything — higher moments, alternative
multiplicity distributions, explicit time dependence, whatever. For a
methodology paper this might actually be cleaner than wrapping OpenMC.

interesting enough to be worth resolving carefully, or whether the bulk of
the variation is captured by source position and everything else is small
corrections. You probably get this done in 2-3 weeks if you're not
interrupted.

If the structure is rich enough to warrant proper treatment, move to Step 2

Not in the way I think you're hoping, and it's worth being precise about why
because the question is exactly the right one to ask.

Bell's equation is a forward equation in the sense that, given a
configuration (geometry, materials, cross-sections, fission multiplicity
distributions), it computes the extinction probability — equivalently POI —
as a function of source phase space. Forward in this context means
physics-to-statistics: you specify what the system *is* and the equation
tells you what it *does* statistically.

What you're asking is the inverse: given an observed time series of
detected neutrons (the .lmx file), can Bell's equation tell you what the
source phase-space distribution was? That's a fundamentally different
problem. You're asking the equation to run statistics-to-physics, and the
answer is that Bell's equation alone cannot do that — but the problem is
solvable through a different framework that uses Bell as a component.

The reason Bell can't be inverted directly is that the equation doesn't
have a unique inverse. Many different source distributions and
configurations can produce statistically indistinguishable detection time
series. The mass-efficiency degeneracy in Walston et al. is exactly this:
their forward model (the Prasad–Snyderman waiting-time distribution, which
is built from the same generating-function machinery as Bell) produces
nearly identical predictions for many (m_S, ε) pairs at fixed k_eff. The
information just isn't in the data.

What you can do — and what Walston et al. actually do — is treat this as a
Bayesian inverse problem. The recipe is:

You have a forward model that maps configuration parameters Θ to a
predicted distribution of detection times: Pr[τ | Θ]. This is the
Prasad–Snyderman inter-arrival distribution (their Eq. 119), and it's built
from Bell-derived chain multiplicity distributions.

You have observed inter-arrival times {τ_1, τ_2, …, τ_M} from the .lmx
file.

from that specific event may differ from the average. FREYA-with-delayed
